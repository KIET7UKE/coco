## Container hosting platform on Coco: the workload in practical terms

A container is the unit of deploy that survives a change of cloud. If your app is an image, you can leave a PaaS that only accepts buildpacks. Docker hosting should mean: give us the image, we run it, we put it on a private network, we terminate TLS if it is public. It should not mean you now operate a cluster. Kubernetes is optional. Most products need one web container, one worker container, and a database. Coco runs those containers on its own servers and prices additional usage at $0.015 per vCPU hour after $20 in monthly credits.

## Container hosting platform performance on Coco

Coco's practical performance argument is capacity. The platform often makes 24 GB of RAM, 24 vCPU, and as many as 48 replicas available within $20 of monthly credits. Memory gives databases, runtimes, caches, and model-serving processes room to work; replicas let independent requests proceed without waiting behind one overloaded process.

Those resources should still be tested against the real application. A serious Container hosting platform evaluation measures memory pressure, CPU saturation, queue depth, and tail latency before increasing every limit. Coco makes a large resource envelope available, but efficient sizing remains the best way to preserve both speed and budget.

### Container hosting platform and the infrastructure behind Coco

Coco runs workloads on high-grade infrastructure that typically uses Intel Xeon or AMD EPYC processors. A standard host can carry 192 physical cores, while NVMe storage can deliver roughly 1 GB/s to 10 GB/s depending on the device and workload. In a Container hosting platform review, that combination matters because CPU contention and storage wait time can make a generously sized virtual service feel slow.
